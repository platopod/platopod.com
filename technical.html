How it works.

One camera, small robots and a computer that builds a world around them. There is little to set up and little to go wrong in front of a class, and your own code can drive it in any language.

The kit

The pods, a camera and a projector on one tripod, and a small computer. There are no markers to lay out, no walls to build, no tracking system to install and no cables to the robots.

  • Switch onThe box starts by itself and opens its own Wi-Fi. The console is a web page on that network.
  • CalibrateOne pod drives a small circle, about half a minute. From its path the system works out how the camera sits over the table.
  • DrawClick two corners of a rectangle on the screen, or trace another shape, and that is the arena. The pods never cross its edge, although it exists only in software. If the camera is knocked, the pods stop until the arena has been checked.

The pods

Each pod is a round robot 85 mm across, with two wheels, a small rechargeable battery and a marker on its lid that the camera reads. It is kept simple by design: it takes wheel commands over Bluetooth, reports how far its wheels have turned, and stops if the commands stop arriving. The intelligence is on the server, where it can be read, changed and tested.

Seeing the arena

One overhead camera reads the marker on every pod and turns it into a position and a heading, many times a second. Nothing else goes on the table. From the same picture the console draws a top-down map at a constant scale, a picture for the projector and a 3D view for the big screen.

The console, in 3D

The console opens in 3D in any browser: the pods as they are built, the ground in relief, sensors with their reach. Place things and draw routes on the 3D ground, or switch to the map. On the Mission sheet you draw the exercise itself: zones, cover, sensors, start positions, a gas release, lines of tape, each dragged, sized and turned, and in force at once.

A world around the pods

Real pods and virtual units share one world, and an exercise cannot tell them apart. The server simulates what the robots carry and what surrounds them.

  • SensorsTwelve, simulated: gas with a real detector's lag, GNSS, sonar, radar, lidar, thermal, rangefinder, IED detector, direction finding, UAV camera, friend-or-foe and a line sensor over tape drawn on the map. Each pod's set, and each sensor's noise, can be changed while an exercise runs.
  • Ground and radioThe ground's heights come from real elevation data at the exercise's scale, and a slope slows a pod. Radio links fade with distance and terrain and can be jammed.
  • EngagementEvery shot is resolved against the weapon, line of sight, cover, visibility and the target's armour. Damage slows a unit and degrades its fire.
  • Rules and opposing forceWeapons control and the rules of engagement are checked before each shot. The opposing force needs no operator. Both run on a classroom box set up for the tactical exercises, not yet in the hosted rooms.
  • UmpireGas releases, wind shifts and strikes are injected mid-exercise and show on sensors and maps straight away.
  • SafetyWhatever drives it, every command is checked by the server first: a pod is stopped short of the arena's edge, a declared obstacle and other pods, to within the camera's tracking accuracy. A pod that hears no command for 0.6 s stops.

Your own code, any language

Everything a program does, from driving a pod to reading a sensor, travels over one web connection as plain messages, so any language can drive the arena. Libraries for Python, C, Java and JavaScript make it a few lines, and one program can drive a whole swarm of pods. This one joins a demonstration room and drives a pod in a square, reading where it really is after every step.

Python

import math, time
from platopod import Arena

arena, pod = Arena.join_room("TLQGNR", "Ada")   # the room's code, and your name

for side in range(4):
    start = pod.pose
    while math.dist((start.x, start.y), (pod.pose.x, pod.pose.y)) < 0.25:
        pod.drive(0.1, 0.0)                       # forward at 10 cm/s
        time.sleep(0.1)
    heading = start.theta + math.pi / 2           # a quarter turn left
    while abs(math.remainder(heading - pod.pose.theta, math.tau)) > 0.02:
        pod.drive(0.0, 0.8)                       # turn on the spot
        time.sleep(0.1)
pod.stop()

The documentation has a guide to the Python library and a notebook for each lesson.

A real pod has a narrow range of speeds, so the server fits each command to what that pod can do.

In your room or hosted

The same software runs on a box in your room, on its own Wi-Fi, or on our server, several arenas at once, reached from any browser. A laptop in the room can carry a webcam's view of real pods up to the hosted arena; that part is being tested. The exercise also reaches ATAK, laid over a real map, from the box or through our hosted TAK server.

Built on ROS 2, with over three thousand automated tests. Engineering details are available on request.
