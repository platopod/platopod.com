How Plato Pod is built.

Small robots, one camera and a mini PC that builds a world around them. There is little to set up and little to go wrong. Everything a student or an instructor touches goes through one interface, and any piece can be replaced without changing the others.

Little to set up

The kit is the pods, a camera and a projector on one tripod, and a mini PC. There are no markers to lay out, no walls to build, no tracking system to install and no cables to the robots. Fewer parts means less to carry into a room, and less to go wrong in front of a class.

  • Switch onThe mini PC starts the platform by itself and opens its own Wi-Fi network. The console is a web page on that network.
  • CalibrateOne pod drives a small circle, which takes about half a minute. From its path the system works out how the camera sits over the table.
  • DrawThe instructor clicks two corners of a rectangle on the screen, or traces another shape, and that is the arena.

Or hosted, with no box at all

The same software runs on a server: several arenas in one ROS 2 graph, each a namespace with its own gateway, and in front of them a small service with accounts and rooms. A room holds an arena per group, and everything reaches it over TLS from an ordinary browser.

The pods

Each pod is a round robot 85 mm across: a 3D-printed body split by two wheel slots, a custom circuit board with an ESP32-S3, two geared motors, and a small lithium battery charged over USB-C. A marker printed on the lid is how the camera finds it.

The pod is kept simple by design. It takes wheel commands over Bluetooth and stops if no command arrives for 600 ms. The overhead camera measures where it goes, and the server steers it from that. The control logic is on the server, where it can be read, changed and tested.

Seeing the arena

One overhead camera reads the marker on every pod and turns it into a position and a heading, many times a second. Nothing else goes on the table: no corner markers, no walls, no mat.

The camera's position over the table is calibrated from a pod's own movement. The pod drives one small circle, and the system fits the table's surface to the path it saw. The instructor then draws the arena over the camera's picture, by clicking two corners of a rectangle; other shapes can be traced corner by corner. The pods stay within the arena and never cross its boundaries, although those boundaries exist only in software. If the camera is knocked or moved, the pods stop until the arena has been checked.

From the same picture the console draws a top-down view at a constant scale, so what the camera sees at an angle can be read like a map.

A world around the pods

Physical pods and virtual units read the same world, and exercise logic cannot tell them apart. The server simulates what the robots carry and what surrounds them.

  • SensorsEleven, simulated: gas with a real detector's lag, GNSS in metres or latitude and longitude, sonar, radar, lidar, thermal, rangefinder, IED detector, direction finding, UAV camera and friend-or-foe. Each pod's set, and each sensor's noise, can be changed while an exercise runs.
  • EngagementEvery shot is resolved against the weapon, line of sight, cover, visibility and the target's armour. Damage slows a unit and degrades its fire, however it is driven.
  • Rules and opposing forceThe weapons control status (hold, tight or free) and the rules of engagement are checked before each shot is resolved. The opposing force needs no operator: it patrols, observes, engages and withdraws.
  • UmpireGas releases, wind shifts and strikes are injected mid-exercise and show on sensors and maps straight away.
  • SafetyWhatever drives it, a pod will not leave the arena, cross a declared obstacle or drive into another robot.

One interface, any language

Everything a client does (driving a pod, reading a sensor, receiving an engagement, injecting an umpire event) travels over one WebSocket as JSON, so code in any language can use the arena. Small libraries in Python, C, Java and JavaScript wrap it for students. Inside the server, the parts exchange typed ROS 2 messages, so any of them can be rewritten in Python, C++, MATLAB, Rust or Java. The exercise also reaches ATAK over Cursor on Target.

How it is run

  • ServerA mini PC with no screen or keyboard, running ROS 2 Jazzy and Python in Docker on an x86 processor, or a hosted server running several arenas, reached over TLS. A GPU is used when present and never required.
  • ConsoleA web page, with nothing to install: one view for the instructor, one for each team, a top-down picture for a projector and a 3D view for the room's big screen, all from one feed.
  • PodsEach pod reports its wheel encoders, and the server fuses them with the camera's fixes, so a pod's position is carried between fixes.
  • TestsOver three thousand unit tests, and a suite that launches the whole system and drives it the way clients do.
